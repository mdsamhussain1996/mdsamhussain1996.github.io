Research

My research lies at the intersection of fractional calculus, dynamical systems theory, and neural network mathematics. I study how complex interconnected systems synchronize, and how fractional-order operators—which encode memory and hereditary effects—can be exploited to understand and control this emergent behaviour. Below I describe my core research threads.

🧠
Primary Research Area

Fractional-Order Quaternion-Valued Neural Networks

The problem. Classical neural network models use real- or complex-valued weights and states. Quaternion-valued networks extend this to four-dimensional hypercomplex space, enabling a single neuron to encode multi-dimensional structured data (e.g., 3-D rotations, colour images). When the network dynamics are governed by a fractional-order derivative—a derivative of non-integer order that captures long-range temporal memory—the resulting system is richer but far harder to analyse than its integer-order counterpart.

My contribution. My PhD thesis (IIT Mandi, 2021–2025, supervised by Prof. Muslim Malik) developed a systematic framework for studying the stability and synchronization of such systems. I established novel Lyapunov-based stability criteria by decomposing quaternion systems into four real-valued sub-systems, avoiding the non-commutativity pitfall of quaternion algebra. The resulting conditions are expressed as linear-matrix inequalities (LMIs) that can be checked efficiently.

What's next. I am extending the framework to stochastic fractional-order quaternion networks driven by Lévy noise, and to architectures with reaction-diffusion terms—capturing spatial heterogeneity in neuromorphic hardware.

Synchronization Theory

Fixed-Time & Projective Synchronization — with an Image-Encryption Application

The problem. Synchronization asks: when does a response system track a drive system's trajectory, regardless of initial conditions? Projective synchronization generalizes this: the response converges to a scaled version of the drive. Fixed-time synchronization adds the requirement that convergence occurs within a settling time that is independent of initial conditions—a critical property for real-time applications.

My contribution. I derived fixed-time and projective synchronization controllers for fractional-order quaternion-valued networks using both feedback and adaptive control strategies. A key application: because synchronized chaotic systems share a secret keystream, I demonstrated a secure image-encryption scheme where the synchronization error acts as the encryption signal. Encryption keys are never transmitted; only the chaotic drive signal is shared.

What's next. I am investigating event-triggered fixed-time synchronization to reduce communication overhead, and exploring robustness guarantees under network attacks.

Control Theory

Controllability of Fractional-Order Systems

The problem. Controllability asks whether a system can be steered from any initial state to any target state by a suitable input in finite time. For fractional-order systems, the semi-group property is lost, making classical controllability arguments inapplicable. The challenge intensifies with impulsive effects (sudden state jumps) or stochastic disturbances.

My contribution. Using fixed-point theorems (Schauder, Krasnoselskii) and operator-theory methods, I derived sufficient conditions for exact and approximate controllability of fractional-order integro-differential, stochastic, and impulsive systems in Banach spaces, in collaboration with Prof. Juan J. Nieto (University of Santiago de Compostela) and Prof. Dumitru Baleanu (Lebanese American University).

What's next. I am applying these ideas to LLM-negotiation swarms—networks of large-language-model agents whose consensus state can be modelled as a nonlinear fractional-order dynamical system—to understand when such swarms are provably steerable to a desired collective decision.

Impulsive Systems

Controllability of Second-Order Impulsive Systems

The problem. Many engineering systems—mechanical vibrations, population models with harvesting, electrical circuits with switching—are modelled by second-order differential equations subject to impulsive perturbations (instantaneous state resets). Controlling such systems requires results that account for both the continuous dynamics and the impulsive moments.

My contribution. Using cosine family operator theory and compact semigroup arguments, I established controllability criteria for second-order impulsive differential equations in abstract Banach spaces. The results unify and improve several existing results in the literature by relaxing compactness assumptions and incorporating non-local initial conditions.

What's next. Extensions to fractional second-order impulsive systems and networked impulsive control with random impulsive times are in preparation.

Future Directions

Stochastic fractional neural networks: Extending synchronization theory to quaternion-valued networks driven by Brownian motion and Lévy jump processes.
Reaction-diffusion neural networks: Incorporating spatial coupling terms to model neuromorphic hardware and biological neural tissue.
Event-triggered control: Designing aperiodic control laws that achieve fixed-time synchronization with minimal communication overhead.
LLM-negotiation swarms: Applying fractional-order consensus theory to understand the controllability of networks of language-model agents.
Optimal control & learning: Integrating reinforcement-learning policies with fractional-order system constraints for data-driven control of complex networks.
